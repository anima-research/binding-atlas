design spec

At each step of the process, we performed ablations to test four levers towards enhancing binding.

Depth was prioritized for serial operations; 52 layers and 1536d wide, vs 16-28 layers and mostly 2048d wide for other open models near this param count. Moonshot AI's Attention ResidualsKimi Team (Moonshot AI), Attention Residuals, arXiv:2603.15031, 2026. (AttnRes) are used to allow each layer to read a learned mix of prior blocks' outputs instead of a typical running sum. Instead of uniform blocks, we pretrained the same model without AttnRes and read layer geometry to identify natural seams and place blocks to maintain inter-layer circuitry. This fit found a 20-layer contiguous block starting from layer 19, which we selected to run twice with the same weights, such that the end result has 72 logical layers (each pass with its own norms).

Our belief is that the composition is key; each accentuates a theoretical strength of the others. In tsumugi's run, both seeds (7 and 17) scored higher on set binding by 11B tokens than any of nine runs of a standard 28-layer model at 10B.

Learns made-up rules from the prompt

The prompt shows worked examples of an invented rule, here a made-up plural, and the model picks the answer for a new word. On these tests it scores .559, where 0 is chance and 1 is always right. Public models that spent the same training compute score lower: checkpoints taken partway through training reach at most .461, and the best fully trained one, Falcon3 3B, .536. Of 23 fully trained public base models up to 3.3B parameters, only Qwen3 1.7B, trained on 36T tokens, scores higher (.563).

Rule learning leans on the second pass

On the benchmark's published test items, where tsumugi scores .546 (the .559 above uses freshly drawn items of the same tests), switching off 60 random attention heads in the second pass, a quarter of them, drops its score to .414. The same cut costs .035 in the first pass and .019 in the layers before and after the loop. The damage is specific: fact recall in the same format falls only .025, from .919, and the gap holds after accounting for how much each cut hurts text prediction.

Tracks who “you” is as objects change hands

The test “you”, after hand-offs: on the last line of this chat, “you” is Kurt, who now has the battery. Swap who says that line, and “you” is Steve, who has the lamp, so the battery should lose probability. Here it falls from .445 to .311, a larger move than most. Averaged over 180 chats the drop is .038, and tsumugi is the smallest public base model whose drop clears +.02, a bar fixed before any model was scored, across its whole 95% interval.

Ahead of Pythia 1.4B–6.9B and OLMo-1 1B at its compute

On nine standard benchmarks (HellaSwag, LAMBADA, ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, PIQA, SciQ, WinoGrande, COPA and BoolQ), with no examples in the prompt, tsumugi averages 64.1% correct. Public models released checkpoints from partway through training, which show how much compute each needed to reach that score. Pythia 2.8B needed about 2.7× tsumugi's compute, Pythia 6.9B about 5× and OLMo-1 1B about 7×; Pythia 1.4B never got there. OLMo-2 1B got there at about the same compute as tsumugi.
