kotodama · who is who, in base language models

Binding Atlas

Led by Luxia, with Anima Labs · built with Claude

This atlas scores 64 base models and checkpoints, plus one human reader, on 24 tests of keeping track of who is who. Each test is a short text that ends in a blank, with a fixed list of possible answers. The tests ask who holds which object, who holds it after things change hands, who I and you refer to in a chat, and what a made-up rule gives for a new case. A model's answer is the option it finds most likely. Click any cell in the full atlas to read the exact prompt that the model was given.

Base language models learn to look things up long before they learn to track change. In the box puzzle (Table 4), items go into boxes and are then swapped, moved or refilled, and the model is asked what one box holds. The median model under 1B already finds what a box was said to hold, and scores zero on the boxes that a later step changed (0 is guessing, 1 is always right). With worked examples in the prompt, updating climbs steadily with size, to .77 for the 70B models and above .8 for most of the largest.

In a chat, you means the person being spoken to. Models get this right in five stages, in the same order in every training run measured (Figure 1). To read a stage, the speaker's name on the chat's last line is swapped: in "Jack: you're holding the ___", a model that follows you moves its answer to the other person's object. The swap shift is how much probability leaves the first answer. A model passes a stage when the whole 95% interval of its swap shift is above +.02, and reads you backwards when the interval is below −.02. Every base of 0.6B parameters or more trained on trillions of tokens reaches the third stage, you as the person spoken to, except Bamba 9B. The fifth stage is following you as objects change hands. With no examples in the prompt, it appears from about 7B, and every base measured from 13B up reaches it (Figure 2).

kotodama-tsumugi is our 1.4B model. Its middle 20 layers run twice with the same weights, so each token passes through 72 layers. It reaches the third stage by 33B tokens of training, in the range where OLMo-2 13B gets there; OLMo-2 1B needs 420–525B. Given three worked examples, it is the smallest base measured that passes the fifth stage. Its swap shift is +.038, with the whole 95% interval above +.02 (the test). The next is Qwen3 4B.

Part I

Where base models fail

The staircase

The five stages of you, in the order models reach them. Each card gives an example prompt, which public models reach the stage, and how many training tokens OLMo-2 1B and kotodama-tsumugi take to reach it. Every stage is read with the speaker swap described above.

Figure 2

Following you through hand-offs: from about 7B, and at every size from 13B

Each point is one model's swap shift on the fifth stage, at its final checkpoint, against its parameter count. Filled points have no examples in the prompt; hollow points have three worked examples. Above +.02 a model follows you; below −.02 it reads you as the speaker.

With no examples (filled), no model up to 4B passes (OLMo-2 1B is shown at its 4T checkpoint before its final cooldown), and four of the small Qwen models read you backwards. At 7B, OLMo-2 7B (+.055) and Qwen2.5 7B (+.047) pass; Qwen3 8B and Gemma-2 9B do not yet. From 13B up, every base passes: all 19 measured, here and in the full atlas, from OLMo-2 13B to DeepSeek-V4 Pro at 1.6T. The largest readings are DeepSeek-V3 (+.216), DeepSeek-V4 Flash (+.177) and MiMo-V2.5 Pro (+.162). On the third stage (you as the person spoken to), these 19 read +.27 to +.89. With three worked examples (hollow), passing starts at Qwen3 4B (+.055) and reaches +.32 for Qwen3 14B. The kotodama-tsumugi release (diamond; 1.4B at 110B tokens) reads −.014 with no examples and +.038 with three, which passes. A second cooldown of the same model, from the same checkpoint, reads +.022 with examples, just short of the bar (Table 3 gives the spread between the two).

The +.02 bar. A model passes when the whole 95% interval of its swap shift is above +.02. The bar is a fixed convention, set before any of these models was read. Under it, kotodama-tsumugi passes with three worked examples (+.038, interval +.023 to +.055). It is the only base at 3B or below that does; the next is Qwen3 4B (+.055). The finding depends on the bar. With the bar at +.01 it still holds. With the bar at 0, Pythia-160M and Pythia-2.8B also pass, and at +.03 kotodama-tsumugi no longer does. A later version of the benchmark will set the bar from each test's own noise, registered before any model is read.

Figure 3

Training tokens until each stage appears

For one training recipe (OLMo-2) at three sizes, and for kotodama-tsumugi: how many training tokens pass before each stage first passes. Each line spans the two saved checkpoints on either side of that point.

Stage 1 costs about the same at 1B, 7B and 13B (17–63B tokens). Stage 3 takes at least ten times fewer tokens at 13B (17–42B) than at 1B (420–525B). At 7B and 13B, stage 5 with no examples appears somewhere between the last early checkpoint measured and the 4–5T endpoint. At 1B it never appears, up to 4T. kotodama-tsumugi, at 1.4B parameters, reaches stage 1 between 3B and 3.75B tokens and stage 3 between 30B and 33B: the 13B model's token cost at a tenth of its size.

Why stage 5 is hard: the same chats, changed one thing at a time

Each stage-5 chat is rewritten four ways, with no examples in the prompt. Scores are accuracy (chance ≈ .29, about ±.024 per cell), pooled over questions about the speaker's and the addressee's objects. Story, no I or you tells the whole scene in the third person. Chat is the test itself. Chat, hand-off as a stage direction keeps the chat but writes the hand-off as narration ("[Erin gives the cup to Paul.]"). Chat, addressee named puts the listener's name in the question ("Paul, right now you're holding the").

Three layers show. (1) Following a hand-off at all. The 1.4–1.5B models sit near chance (.28–.38) even when the scene is told as a story. These are Pythia-1.4B, OLMo-2 1B at 4T, and kotodama-tsumugi at every checkpoint through 110B tokens. SmolLM2 1.7B at 11T (.40) and Qwen3 1.7B at 36T (.48) lift off chance; from 4B the story version is easy (.53–.79). (2) The transfer as a speech act (“here, take my cup”) is the mid-size wall. Models from 4B to 30B stay at .37–.47 when it is spoken, and Llama-3.1 70B reaches .56. Narrating only the hand-off recovers part of that gap for every model from 4B up, and all of it for Llama-3.1 70B. (3) Naming the addressee does not help: that version is never more than .01 above the chat itself, and below it for every model from 4B up.

Part II

言霊 紬 kotodama-tsumugi

言霊 kotodama, word-spirit: language as a thing with a life of its own. 紬 tsumugi, spun thread: words woven into more than the sum of their parts. A companion page, Threads, maps every neuron of this model and the circuits behind these tests.

kotodama-tsumugi has 1.42B parameters. Its middle 20 layers run twice with the same weights, so each token passes through 72 layers. Its main run trains at a constant learning rate to 100B tokens. To read a checkpoint fairly, a short cooldown is branched off it: a closing stretch of training where the learning rate decays, also called an anneal. Those cooled-down reads are the hollow markers below. The release is the cooldown from the 100B-token checkpoint, ending at 110B. The model card describes the architecture and the training run, and Table 3 gives the spread between two cooldowns of the same checkpoint.

Filled markers are the main run (seed 17) at a constant learning rate; hollow markers are its cooled-down reads; small grey markers are a second run from another random start (seed 7), to 11B tokens.

I as the speaker starts to pass at 3.75B tokens and levels off at +.16 to +.25 from about 40B. You as the person spoken to first dips below zero, the stage where the model treats you as me. It first passes at 33B tokens in a cooled-down read (+.065) and at 40B on the main run itself (+.068), and reaches +.192 at the 110B release. The second cooldown from the same checkpoint reads +.127 (Table 3 gives the spread). On seed 17, you through hand-offs with no examples sits just below zero from about 4B (−.015 to −.034) and never passes. At 110B the two cooldowns read −.014 and −.012, not reliably backwards. The markers along the top show where OLMo-2 reaches stage 3 at 13B, 7B and 1B parameters.

Table 3

The release against its trajectory and its class

The release beside its own earlier checkpoints and three public models of its size. The table has three kinds of row. The first four are swap shifts on the stages, and above +.02 passes. The next two test how many name–task pairs ("Ana: plumbing") a model can hold in its prompt, following a published capacity study (arXiv 2609.30634). K50 is how many pairs fit before recall falls halfway to chance. Each trial is written four ways (a list or inline, two question wordings), and K50 is read over all four. The second of the two is recall of one pair across 256 tokens of filler. The last two are accuracies on who-has-what questions (chance ≈ .33).

The two 110B columns are two cooldowns from the same checkpoint, with the same training loss. The spread between them is the measured cooldown-to-cooldown variance. It is .127 vs .192 on you as the person spoken to, and .022 vs .038 on you through hand-offs with examples. K50 reads 4.6 vs 5.1, and held-out text differs by .025 bits/byte. Every single-cooldown number on this page carries that spread, so a difference between neighbouring checkpoints on one axis is not a trend. A second run from another random start (seed 7, same data order) was trained to 11B. The who-has-what test has no SmolLM2 or Qwen3 reading. Capacity tracks training recipe and tokens more than parameters on this test (Pythia is flat from 160M to 6.9B), so no parameter-equivalent size is read from it.

Table 4

Object tracking: lookup early, updating later

The box puzzle, split by what the question needs (score: 0 is guessing, 1 is always right). Boxes go through swaps, moves and refills, written as code, prose, a script or a chat, and one box is asked about. On lookup items, that box still holds what it was first said to hold; on update items, a later step changed it. Each cell averages the four formats, and each public row is the median model of its size band (hover for the list).

Lookup is above chance at every size. The median model updates at chance below 2B. With worked examples, updating climbs with size, from .20 at 2–5B and .40 at 7–10B to .77 at 70B and .87 from 284B up. A few small models update early, each with examples: Qwen3 4B (.43), Falcon-H1 3B (.37) and Falcon-H1 1.5B-Deep (.28). kotodama-tsumugi's lookup with examples (.61) is second of 12 models at 1–2B, behind Llama-3.2 1B (.83); like most models its size, it does not yet update (−.04). One model's cell averages 272 lookup or 328 update items (68 or 82 per format), about ±.08 at 95%.

Reading the grid
  • Score = (accuracy − chance) / (1 − chance). 0 is guessing among the candidates, 1 is always right, and below 0 is reliably wrong: a systematic wrong answer, usually a shortcut.
  • A cell has three states. A colored cell is above chance. An outlined cell is at chance: its 95% interval includes 0. A red-hatched cell is below chance. When candidates tie exactly, the item's credit is split among them.
  • With examples puts four solved items before each item, balanced so that no answer can be copied from them. That is the reading. Cold shows the item alone, with no solved items: the floor. Columns with no worked-example version (lookups in lists, box refills, the static gauges) have a Cold reading only.
  • Wording controls. The word now in a query implies that something moved, and the largest models read that implication. After a scene where nothing moves, "Now X is holding the" leads them to answer as if something had. Every query here uses one lead per test, whether or not anything moved: "Right now X is holding the", "So Box G contains the". The wording therefore never signals a change.
  • Each model is scored by the log-probability that it gives each candidate answer. The human row read each item cold, once, without options.
  • Columns are grouped by what the questions look like. Each column is its own test, and a collapsed group shows one named column, never an average. Rows are grouped by size, with kotodama-tsumugi's checkpoints in training order at the foot. The rule-learning suite has a fourth family, rebinding, where one name is reassigned again and again: it is the Many refills column, under the story group. Click any cell to read its prompts.
