Across prompt formats

246 neurons hold their place across prompt formats

Wiring is not causal role. 39/3306 is wired like 19/3306, yet removing it does nothing to binding. Every claim drawn from the graph carries this caveat.

246 neurons stay in a top-100 set when the items come with four balanced worked examples: 133 interferers and 134 supporters, from 315 of 1,400 places. Of these, 21 support one task and interfere with another. The circuits above come from bare items, with no worked examples. Attribution was run again on the same items in the new format. For each of seven battery tasks, the top 100 supporters and the top 100 interferers were compared between the two formats.

The wiring among them holds at about twice the rate of unrelated maps. Attribution also scores each link between two of these neurons: how much one neuron's activity feeds the other's effect on the answer. The links among the 246 were measured for attribute binding, dialogue and code variables. Between 23% and 27% of them pass the same test in both formats: same sign, and strength above the task's median. Two unrelated maps would give 12.5%. For dialogue and code variables, the links are about as stable across formats as between two halves of one format's items. Attribute binding rewires: its links have a rank correlation of .17 across formats, against .33 to .47 between two halves of one format. Whole maps move more. Under balanced worked examples, the maps for attribute binding, dialogue, adjacency and code mutation change well beyond their own noise. The maps for code variables and answer change about as much as their noise allows.

The headline

Rule learning and fact recall run on separate circuits

tsumugi learns rules from its prompt and recalls facts in the same prompt format with two different sets of neurons. Removing either set takes out its own skill and leaves the other one standing.

Loading the knock-outs…

  • The rule circuit: 100 neurons, found by attribution on fresh made-up-operator items. Removing them at half dose cuts made-up operators by .067, nearly a quarter of the score (AUC .294 → .227), and pattern completion by .041. Fact recall is unchanged (−.002).
  • The recall circuit: 100 neurons, found the same way on fact items. Removing them at half dose cuts fact recall by .030, and leaves made-up operators unchanged (+.001).
  • The control: random sets 32 times larger, chosen to do as much or more damage to next-token prediction, move neither score by more than .011.
  • How separate: across all 294,912 neurons the two attribution maps are uncorrelated (Spearman .01), and the two top-100 sets share 5 neurons.
  • Where: 40 of the rule circuit's 100 neurons are in the loop's second pass. Its top neuron is 39/3306, the second-pass copy of 19/3306, which broadcasts to more neurons than any other in the model.
  • The rule circuit carries pattern completion too, but not made-up grammar (−.002).

The same amount removed from the first pass costs .035, and outside the loop .019. Who you is, with no examples

Static you runs through the model's two biggest broadcasters

The two neurons that most oppose static you are 19/3306 and 8/797. Out of all 294,912 neurons, they are the first and second by how many neurons they send to. Attribution and the wiring graph, two independent methods, land on the same two.

  • The interferers: removing the 100 interferers raises the Atlas cell from .49 to .85.
  • The supporters: removing the 100 supporters drops it to .12, and the static-you gauge falls from +.192 to +.014, below the binding line.
  • The control: at half dose, both effects stand far outside random sets 32 times larger. The interferers move κ by +.234, against −.013 to −.022 for random sets that do the same damage. The supporters move it by −.312, against −.029 to −.062 for the largest random sets tried, which do a little less damage.
  • This circuit is stable when the wording changes, but not under worked examples: with four examples in the prompt, a different set of neurons does the work (overlap 14 of 100).

Learning from three examples

Three worked examples switch on a circuit for dynamic you

With no examples, tsumugi doesn't track you after objects change hands (−.014). With three, it does (+.038, 95% interval +.023 to +.055). The neurons whose support rises with the examples carry that gain:

  • The knock-out: removing the 100 that rise most halves the gain at half dose (+.038 → +.016). Random sets 32 times larger, with as much or more damage, leave it at +.036 to +.040. At full dose the gain is gone (−.005), with more general damage.
  • Where: 75% of these neurons sit in the looped core, against 56% of all neurons; 43 of 100 are in the second pass. The attention head whose contribution rises most is 39:0, the first position of the second pass.
  • Reuse: 8 of the 100 neurons that support it most with three examples are also static-you supporters (chance: 0, at most 2), so the examples borrow a little of the static-you machinery, not most of it.

exploratory · not registered

Content neurons read clearly, wiring neurons don't

This finding was not registered. The rule circuit is the hardest set of neurons to read. Its steering reliability ranks at the 0.5th percentile against random neurons at the same depths, and how much its firing concentrates on one token ranks at the 1.7th. It looks like wiring. The neurons that support a task read as that task's content:

  • holding neurons, whose J-lens readout is “handheld, wield, hands”;
  • second-person neurons that fire on “you”;
  • a currency neuron in the recall circuit.
Limits

What didn't hold

  • No seesaw: there is no general trade-off between rule and recall. Removing the recall circuit doesn't raise rule learning (+.001).
  • Static you isn't format-stable: under worked examples, a different set of neurons does the work, which is why it doesn't lead this page.
  • Dynamic you is small: the effect is +.038 in absolute terms.
  • Damage at full dose: the full-dose knock-outs do more general damage than same-size random sets. The damage-matched controls above are at half dose, where they pass.

Fluid in-context learning, item by item

Who said what, in held-out scenes

Removing 702 interferers moves who said what in held-out multi-speaker scenes. The margin for the right speaker rises by +.33 nats (95% interval +.16 to +.50). The 702 are the union of the interferer sets chosen on bare items of ten binding tasks. Random sets of the same size move the margin by under .02. Random sets 8 to 15 times larger, matched to the union's damage at half dose, reach at most +.08 at full dose. At half dose the union's effect is small (+.05), and one of those large random sets matches it.

The looped core

One neuron, two passes

Neuron 3306 at position 19 is a causal hub against attribute binding on pass 1. Setting it alone to its average raises the attribute margin by +.57 nats with balanced worked examples, and by +.34 on bare items. Ten random single neurons at the same position move it by .013 or less. With balanced examples, removing it slightly lowers the dialogue and code-variable margins (−.014 and −.016). Positions 19–38 and 39–58 run the same weights, so every neuron of the looped core has one copy on each pass. The pass-2 copy at position 39 has no measurable effect: it moves each task's margin by .013 or less. Attribution had ranked both copies among the neurons with the strongest links to later neurons, and the knock-out separates the two.

A check on the map

Hand-validated neurons, and where the map put them

These neurons were characterised by hand before the map existed (firing screens, steering, forked log-probabilities). The map was built without those results. The loop gives a free test: a neuron's two passes share weights, so if the map tracks function, the two copies should land in the same group.

Method, and what to trust

How it was made

Firing pass24M tokens of the model's training data through all 72 × 4,096 neurons; the 24 strongest and weakest firings of each, and running means.
Three readingsSentence embeddings of each neuron's firing contexts; the tokens that its output vector promotes (logit lens and J-lens); the words that appear when it is steered.
Map and names400 groups by k-means on the embeddings, a 2-D layout by UMAP, then language-model agents named each group from its exemplars and graded the agreement of the readings.
Binding attributionFor 4,440 binding-battery items, each shown bare with no worked examples, activation × gradient of the margin between the right candidate and the strongest wrong one, for every neuron at every position.
Knock-outsSets chosen on training items, tested on held-out items: each neuron's activation replaced by its corpus mean. Controls are random neurons at the same positions.
